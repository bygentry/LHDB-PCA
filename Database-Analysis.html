An Investigation of Primate Life History Data Quality

Author

Gentry Miller

Introduction

“Given this general importance of life history data and with the scientific ideals of transparency and repeatability in mind, one would assume that researchers have long since agreed upon life history definitions and are using reliable and accurate data stored in standardized repositories. However, while various databases exist, standards for data quality and specification are lacking.” (Borries et al. 2013)

Life history theory has provided not just a framework to understand how organisms maximize fitness, but a lexicon of variables that represent a shared currency across organisms. Key variables have emerged to characterize growth (weaning age ), reproduction (age at sexual maturity, first reproduction), and duration of reproduction (lifespan). Despite the widespread use of such measures, researchers face consistent methodological challenges in collating life history data for comparative analyses including access to a substantial range, differences in definitions, discrepancies in data quality, and decision trees on which variables to use and how. 

Here we present a guide that addresses these and other issues for primatologists who want to employ life history data. We review the characteristics of five databases that contain life history data on primates. We also present a frequently advocated approach to summarize variation across traits using dimension reduction analysis, with which we summarize data here. 

The databases assessed here are the PanTHERIA (Jones et al. 2009), AnAge (de Magalhães & Costa 2009), Amniotes (Myrhvold et al. 2015), Ernest (2003), and Kappeler & Pereira (2003) databases on life history statistics.

Preliminaries

Data Cleaning

Filtering Primate Data

Four of the databases under investigation (all but Kappeler & Pereira (2003)) include data for more animals than primates - our focus is on the quality of primate life history data, so all other taxa were excluded from our tests.

Missing Values and Point Correction

Our .csv of the Kappeler & Pereira database was transcribed by hand from a digital scan of the original paper publication. One value of adult female body mass (BM.F) in the Kappeler & Pereira data has a “?”, making the object type ‘character’ instead of ‘numeric’ . Mixing number and non-number characters would result in a forced NA for that value when converting to type numeric, so the ‘?’ is removed to include the value in completeness tests despite its uncertainty. Additionally, several of the databases use -999 as the “missing value” - these are converted to NA for all fields of type numeric to allow quantitative analysis.

Variable Definitions and Selection

Each database records a number of life history statistics, any of which are reported in several of the databases as they are core life history traits (i.e. inter-birth interval, gestation length, etc). See how the variables are defined in each source database here. Rather than evaluating the completeness and reliability of every variable across all databases, we focus on a subset of “key life history traits” that characterize primate reproductive life history and are present in all or most of the databases examined. These traits serve as a representative sample of variables commonly used in taxonomic comparisons in life history research:

Standardizing Units

To make valid comparisons, variables across databases should be in common units. There are several changes to be made to standardize the units to meet these conditions:

  1. Longevity is reported in years

  2. All other time values are reported in days

  3. All mass values are reported in grams

Some of the time values are reported in months - we treat a month as the average length of a month in the Gregorian calendar in days and use these conversion factors throughout unit standardization:

\[ 1 \text{ year } = \frac{365.25 \text{ days}}{1 \text{ year}} = \frac{365.25 \text{ days}}{12 \text{ months}} \approx \frac{30.44 \text{ days}}{1 \text{ month}} \\ \]

The following unit changes are necessary:

Amniotes (Myrhvold et al. 2015):

  • Inter-birth Interval (IBI) should be converted from years to days

AnAge (de Magalhães & Costa 2009):

  • No changes necessary

Ernest (2003):

  • Age at First Reproduction (AFR), Gestation Length (gest_len), and Weaning Age (weaning_age) should be converted from months to days

  • IBI should be derived from litters/year

    • IBI is effectively days/birth (or days/litter) and can be derived from litters/year as such:

      \[ 1 \, \div \frac{x \text{ litters}}{1 \text{ year}} = \frac{x \text{ years}}{1 \text{ litter}} \,\times \, \frac{365.25 \text{ days}}{1 \text{ year}} = \frac{x \text{ days}}{1\text{ litter}} \]

  • Maximum Longevity (max_longevity) should be converted from months to years

PanTHERIA (Jones et al. 2009):

  • Maximum Longevity should be converted from months to years

Kappeler & Pereira (2003):

  • IBI should be converted from months to days
Code 
days_per_year <- 365.25
months_per_year <- 12
days_per_month <- days_per_year/months_per_year

amniote_s <- amniote_s %>% 
  mutate(
    IBI = IBI * days_per_year
    # converts years to days
  )

  mutate(
    AFR = AFR * days_per_month,
    # converts months to days

trait_labels <- c(
  max_longevity = "Lifespan (Years)",
  IBI = "Interbirth Interval (Days)",
  AFR = "Age at First Reproduction (Days)",
  gest_len = "Gestation Length (Days)",
  weaning_age = "Weaning Age (Days)",
  body_mass_u = "Adult Mass - Unspecified (g)",
  body_mass_m = "Adult Mass - Male (g)",
  body_mass_f = "Adult Mass - Female (g)",
  age_at_maturity_u = "Sexual Maturity - Unspecified (Days)",
  age_at_maturity_m = "Sexual Maturity - Male (Days)",
  age_at_maturity_f = "Sexual Maturity - Female (Days)"
)

scope_df <- key_long %>%
  group_by(source, trait_raw) %>%
  summarise(
    n_total = n(),
    n_nonmissing = sum(!is.na(value)),
    prop_missing_panel = mean(is.na(value)),
    .groups = "drop"
  ) %>%
  mutate(
    in_scope = (n_nonmissing / n_total) >= 0.01,
    prop_missing_within_scope = if_else(in_scope, prop_missing_panel, NA_real_),
    trait = recode(trait_raw, !!!trait_labels)
  )

scope_summary <- scope_df %>%
  group_by(source) %>%
  summarise(
    scope_coverage = mean(in_scope),                              
    panel_completeness = 1 - mean(prop_missing_panel),            
    within_scope_completeness = 1 - mean(prop_missing_within_scope, na.rm = TRUE),
    .groups = "drop"
  )

# One-tailed Permutation Test

# create list of sex dependent variable names for checking sex differentiation
sex_dep_vars <- c("Sexual Maturity - Female (Days)", "Sexual Maturity - Male (Days)", "Sexual Maturity - Unspecified (Days)", "Adult Mass - Female (g)", "Adult Mass - Male (g)", "Adult Mass - Unspecified (g)")

# create a new df for the permutation test
## completeness - the inverse proportion of missing values
## sexed - a boolean representing a trait is stratified by sex
perm_df <- scope_df %>% 
  filter(in_scope) %>% 
  mutate(
    completeness = 1 - prop_missing_panel,        # inverse proportion of missingness for each trait
    sexed = trait %in% sex_dep_vars         # boolean: TRUE if variable is sex stratified, else FALSE 
  )

# creates a function for the permutation test
perm_test <- function(df, n = 10000, seed = 1){
  set.seed(seed)

  x <- df$completeness
  sexed <- df$sexed

  n_sexed <- sum(sexed)
  n_non <- sum(!sexed)

  if(n_sexed == 0 || n_non == 0){
    return(tibble(
      mean_sexed = NA_real_,
      mean_non = NA_real_,
      obs_diff = NA_real_,
      p_value = NA_real_,
      n_sexed = n_sexed,
      n_non = n_non
    ))
  }

  mu_sexed <- mean(x[sexed])
  mu_non <- mean(x[!sexed])
  obs <- mu_non - mu_sexed

  perm_stats <- replicate(n, {
    perm <- sample(sexed)
    mean(x[!perm]) - mean(x[perm])
  })

  tibble(
    mean_sexed = mu_sexed,
    mean_non = mu_non,
    obs_diff = obs,
    p_value = mean(perm_stats >= obs), 
    n_sexed = n_sexed,
    n_non = n_non
  )
}

When completeness is evaluated only among traits that are in the scope of each database, sex-stratified variables are not uniformly less complete. In fact, for Ernest (2003), PanTHERIA (Jones et al. 2009), and Kappeler & Pereira (2003), sex-stratified traits show higher mean completeness than non-sex-stratified traits, reflected by negative observed differences. In contrast, AnAge and the Amniote database show higher completeness for non-sex-stratified traits, although differences are not statistically significant. These results suggest that the apparent incompleteness of sex-stratified variables observed in panel-based analyses is largely driven by scope differences rather than systematic data loss within sex-stratified traits.

When trait scope is taken into account, the apparent incompleteness of sex-stratified variables across databases is largely explained by differences in database design. Databases such as Ernest (2003), PanTHERIA (Jones et al. 2009), and Kappeler & Pereira (2003) do not uniformly report sex-specific measures of adult body mass or age at maturity, which inflates missingness when completeness is assessed across a fixed panel of traits. However, scope-aware analyses show that sex-stratified variables are not consistently less complete than non-sex-stratified variables, and in some cases are more complete. These findings highlight a central trade-off in comparative life-history analyses: sex-specific measures of traits such as age at sexual maturity are biologically informative, but their inconsistent inclusion across databases complicates broad taxonomic comparisons. In contexts where sex-specific maturity data are sparse or structurally unavailable, age at first reproduction may serve as a valid alternative, as it is more widely reported and unambiguously defined.
